EXTRAIT 1:
We conducted our study in a landscape of approximately 45,000 km 2 in the Satpura-Maikal landscape of Central India (21.15 N -76.50 E, 22.80 N -81.05 E). Narmada and Tapti are the major rivers in this landscape, the catchment of which is formed by the Satpura Range in the north and Maikal Range to the east (Krishnan, 1982). The Vindhyachal Range runs parallel and north of the Satpura Range, separated by the Narmada floodplain. Altitude varies from 320 to 1352 msl and topography ranges from plains, undulating plateaus and grasslands to precipitous cliffs. Temperature ranges from 2 °C in the winter to 48 °C in the summer, marked by an intervening monsoon season (June-August) when the area receives 1000-2200 mm of rainfall. Forest types vary from dry deciduous mixed forest, dry deciduous teak (Tectona grandis) forests, to moist peninsular sal (Shorea robusta) forest.
We sampled four leopard populations in five Tiger Reserves (TRs) in two central Indian states: Madhya Pradesh (MP) and Maharashtra (Mh) (Fig. 1). These TRs are Satpura (1428 km 2 , MP), Melghat (1677 km 2 , Mh), Pench (758 km 2 , MP; 258 km 2 , Mh) and Kanha (2059 km 2 , MP). Although Pench TR is physically present in two states (MP and Mh), the leopard population is contiguous between the two states, and we treated it as one unit in this study to make it biologically meaningful. Satpura and Melghat are located on the western edge and Kanha and Pench on the eastern edge of this landscape, and these two pairs of TRs are each inter-connected by a forest corridor (Fig. 1). Satpura is also connected by a corridor to Pench, while there is patchy connectivity between Melghat and Pench. Leopards and their prey species were reported from these corridors (Jhala et al., 2011). The matrix between the eastern and western pair of TRs has some tracts of forests but is highly populated and contains numerous small cities, towns and villages along with dams, mines and other industries. The soil is agriculturally very productive, and large areas of forest have been converted into cultivation.

We identified nine migrants in this landscape. STRUCTURE and GENECLASS produced similar results and identified several of the same individuals (Table 3). STRUCTURE detected six  migrants and GENECLASS detected eight migrants when the two genetic clusters were used as prior population information.
We also ran the analysis with the four TRs and used this analysis to identify which reserve was the source of the putative migrants. We found that six individuals were common to both outcomes, and all three migrants in Satpura TR were assigned to Melghat TR as their source population. STRUCTURE identified 26 individuals of admixed ancestry (Satpura-1, Melghat-13, Pench-7, and Kanha-5), which had q values between 0.2 and 0.8. These individuals could not be definitely identified as either migrants or residents.

EXTRAIT 3:
Aim Habitat loss and fragmentation can influence the genetic structure of biological populations. Large terrestrial predators can often avoid genetic subdivision due to fragmentation because they have high rates of dispersal-mediated gene flow. Leopards (Panthera pardus) are found in a variety of habitats and are the most widely distributed of the large and middle-sized felids. We investigated the genetic diversity and population substructure of leopards (P.p fusca) in a fragmented meta-population comprised of four populations from five protected areas and inter-connecting corridors spread over an area of 45,000 km 2 in central India.Location Kanha, Pench, Satpura and Melghat Tiger Reserves, and interconnecting corridors in the Satpura-Maikal Landscape of Central India.Methods We collected faecal samples and used genetic methods to identify individuals, estimate the genetic variation and evaluate the patterns of genetic substructuring within this meta-population of leopards.
ResultsWe identified 217 individual leopards using a panel of seven microsatellite loci. Leopards showed high levels of genetic diversity in all sampled populations. Spatial and non-spatial Bayesian analysis revealed at least two admixed genetic populations with indications of ongoing genetic subdivision. Genetic differentiation between populations was not explained by geographic distance. We identified nine individuals as migrants, most of which were assigned to reserves connected by corridors.Main conclusions Our study demonstrates that the leopard, an adaptable and vagile species, can become genetically differentiated with increased habitat fragmentation. Contrary to our hypothesis of panmixia, our results indicate that although leopards in this landscape are admixed, there is genetic substructuring at both the landscape and the fine-scale level. We conclude that this is due to habitat fragmentation and corridors are of immense value in maintaining genetic connectivity in this landscape.

EXTRAIT 4:
The significant deviation from HWE for the global test indicated population substructuring, which was further confirmed by the low, but significant F ST values across all comparisons. When comparing pairwise differentiation measures (F ST and D EST ), it is important to note that the highest levels of differentiation observed are between Kanha and Satpura, populations that are not connected by corridors (Fig. 1); and the lowest values are between Kanha and Pench, populations that are connected by a corridor. The appropriate choice of the number of subpopulations, K, was not straightforward for this dataset as we found indications of K = 2 and K = 4. We selected K = 2 as the most parsimonious choice for our dataset based on higher q values, greater geographical concordance, support from spatial and non-spatial approaches, and recommendation from previous studies to choose the smallest value of K that captures the most structure in the data (Pritchard et al., 2000;Pritchard & Wen, 2004). Both STRUCTURE and TESS consistently identified one cluster that consisted of individuals almost exclusively from Satpura, whereas the other cluster consisted of individuals from the other three reserves. However, there is also evidence of ongoing differentiation, based on the second peak for second-order rate of change at K = 4, combined with the high frequency of accurate assignments. The further subdivision of the larger genetic cluster (Kanha-Pench-Melghat) into populations corresponding to geographic locations (Fig. S4) suggests that genetic subdivision is ongoing among leopards in this landscape. Leopards in the entire landscape are admixed, but the Satpura population is genetically more distinct from the other three reserves. Because Satpura is closest to the Narmada River, it could be genetically connected to protected areas across the river that are known to have good leopard populations (Jhala et al., 2011). Leopards could use the several riverine tributaries to move across the river (Athreya, 2006). Alternatively, there could be something specific to the corridor between Melghat and Satpura that prevents higher levels of gene flow. A fine-scale GIS analysis comparing factors such as forest cover, prey availability, road density, human population, land-use patterns and disturbances such as mines and dams is needed to determine which factors are responsible for the patterns that we observe. Although corridors in this landscape have several open-cast coal mines that have existed since as early as 1904 (Gee, 1940) and continue to exist all along the corridor (Singh & Shukla, 2004), we cannot conclusively determine how the Melghat-Satpura corridor differs from the other corridor in this landscape based on our study. The relationship between geographic and genetic distances (F ST and D EST ) among populations was not significant, suggesting absence of isolation by distance in leopard meta-population in our study area and that the observed subdivision cannot be explained by geographic distance between the TRs. Our analysis confirms that the results are not an artefact of sampling scale, nor are they driven by one population. Considering that leopards are habitat generalists (Bailey, 1993) and can disperse over long distances, these results are rather surprising and indicate the role of habitat fragmentation in increasing genetic isolation.
Leopards and predator-occupied forests were abundant in the 19th century in central India, when rewards were given for killing predators (Rangarajan, 1998). Conversion of forest lands into agricultural areas and demand for wood to construct railroads in India escalated the denudation of these Central Highland forests in the 19-20th century, leading to the degree of habitat fragmentation that we observe today (Rangarajan, 1998). Although leopards are often found in disturbed and degraded forests habitats, in close proximity to human habitation, and can survive on small prey like domestic dogs, cats, sheep and goats (Seidensticker et al., 1990;Edgaonkar & Chellam, 2002), given ample medium to large prey and some cover, leopards prefer undisturbed areas distant from human settlements (Ngoprasert et al., 2007;Gavashelishvili & Lukarevskiy, 2008). Leopards tend to avoid areas where tigers live at high densities because tigers are known to kill and out-compete leopards (Schaller, 1967(Schaller, , 1972;;Seidensticker, 1976;McDougal, 1988;Odden et al., 2010); and opportunistically steal their prey (Seidensticker, 1976). For example, in Sariska TR in western India, leopard densities have substantially increased since tigers were extirpated in 2004 (Chauhan et al., 2005;Mondal et al., 2012). Because tigers avoid human-dominated areas, these areas create a potential competition-free refuge for leopards (Odden et al., 2010). However, ecological escape from competition with other carnivores may lead to leopards coming into direct conflict with humans (Athreya, 2006).
Previous studies show that many other large carnivore species can be genetically differentiated at different levels due to habitat fragmentation. In a concurrent study in this same landscape, Sharma et al. (2012) reported low genetic substructuring of tigers, with lower F ST values, indicating higher gene flow than in leopards. In Tanzania (Spong et al., 2000), leopards had high gene flow and very low genetic differentiation. Pumas in Wyoming (Anderson et al., 2004) and Brazil (Miotto et al., 2011) did not have significant population substructuring, whereas Ernest et al. (2003) found significant genetic structure in California where urbanization has severely reduced and isolated natural habitats for the species. Jaguars were found to have high genetic structure and very low migration rates in the Upper Parana Atlantic forest due to severe habitat fragmentation over the past 30 years (Haag et al., 2010). Ultimately, even in highly vagile species, it appears that genetic differentiation varies by the landscape configuration, intensity of the habitat change and species life-history traits, ecology and behaviour.
Dispersal in the Central Indian leopard metapopulation Wright (1931) and Franklin (1980) projected that one successful migrant per generation would be sufficient to prevent population differentiation caused by genetic drift. More recent work suggests that 1-10 migrants per generation may be necessary (Mills & Allendorf, 1996) or even more than 10 migrants per generation (Vucetich & Waite, 2000). Our assignment tests allowed us to identify at least nine-first-generation migrants in our landscape. We identified three migrants in Satpura, one in Melghat, four in Pench and one in Kanha. Because six of the individuals identified with the four TRs as reference in the analysis were the same as those identified with two genetic clusters, we use the results from the former analysis to assign these migrant individuals to source TRs. Four of these six individuals were assigned to TRs connected by corridors. All three migrants in Satpura were assigned to Melghat, and one migrant in Pench was assigned to Satpura TR. These are pairs of TRs that have interconnecting corridors. Two individuals in Pench had the highest probability of having originated from Melghat, and one migrant in Kanha was assigned with highest probability to Satpura. Eleven per cent of all sampled individuals had admixed ancestry. The presence of a substantial number of admixed individuals in these areas suggests that leopards have been able to move across the landscape and reproduce, at least in the recent past.
