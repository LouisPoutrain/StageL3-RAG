"DNA sampling methods used in wildlife genetic analysis: A case study on non-invasive fecal collection"

The study employed a non-invasive DNA sampling method using fecal samples collected from the study population. Animals were not contacted or manipulated during the sampling process, aligning with the minimal disturbance approach advocated by Taberlet et al. (2018). Fecal samples were collected using sterile gloves and placed in Whirl-Pak bags (Nasco, USA), which were then stored on ice before being transported to the laboratory for analysis.

Fecal collection occurred at weekly intervals over a six-month period, with an average of five samples collected per animal. This frequency was chosen to minimize the impact on individual animals while ensuring sufficient DNA material for genetic analyses. Prior to sampling, the study area was systematically searched for fresh fecal deposits using visual and scent-based detection methods. Once located, fecal samples were carefully collected using clean forceps and placed in sterile bags.

To minimize disturbance to animals and their habitats, all fieldwork was conducted during off-peak activity hours, and care was taken to avoid trampling vegetation or leaving behind any waste materials. All necessary permits were obtained, and the study protocol was approved by relevant ethical committees, ensuring adherence to guidelines for animal welfare and minimal impact on wildlife populations."

Methods:

DNA sampling protocols varied depending on the target species and available resources. For non-invasively collected samples such as feathers or feces, we used barbed wire fences baited with appropriate food items to attract specific species (e.g., fruit for birds). Samples were collected using sterile forceps and placed in labeledзи zip-top tubes containing silica beads to preserve DNA. These samples required no direct handling of animals and thus had minimal impact on their welfare.

For more invasive blood sampling, we used mist nets or walk-in traps depending on the target species' size and behavior. Captured animals were briefly restrained using appropriate techniques for each species to minimize stress and injury. Blood was collected via venipuncture using heparinized microcapillary tubes, with care taken not to exceed 1% of the animal's total blood volume. After sampling, animals were promptly released at the capture site. This protocol, although more invasive than non-contact methods, was designed to cause minimal perturbation to wild populations and comply with ethical guidelines for wildlife research.

In both protocols, strict sterilization procedures were followed to prevent cross-contamination between samples. Additionally, all necessary permits and approvals were obtained prior to conducting fieldwork to ensure compliance with local regulations and ethical considerations.
