In this study, we employed non-invasive genetic sampling methods to collect DNA from wild animal populations. Fecal samples were collected as the primary source of DNA due to their abundance and ease of collection without causing stress or perturbation to the animals (Taberlet et al., 1997). We used barbed wire loops attached to poles to collect fecal samples from well-defined areas within each population's range. Samples were collected once every two weeks over a period of six months to ensure sufficient genetic representation while minimizing the risk of collecting related individuals' feces repeatedly. To preserve DNA integrity, we stored samples in sterile tubes containing lysis buffer and placed them on ice before transporting them to the laboratory for further processing. Ethical considerations included minimizing disturbance to animals' habitats and avoiding collection during mating or breeding seasons to reduce stress and potential disruption of behavioral patterns.
--------------------------------------------------

Document hypothétique 3:
--------------------------------------------------
For non-invasive sampling of wild animals, we focused on collecting faecal samples. We used hair snares baited with appropriate attractants specific to the targeted species to capture hairs bearing skin cells. The equipment consisted of barbless wire nooses attached to trees or other sturdy structures along known animal trails. Once an animal's hair was caught in the noose, it would trigger a device that recorded the event and released the hair after a set time interval. This method minimizes stress and disturbance to the animals as they were not handled or observed during sampling.

We collected faecal samples using non-invasive DNA extraction methods from the captured hairs. The samples were stored at -20°C until further processing. We followed strict ethical guidelines to minimize any potential impact on the animals, including regular monitoring of capture sites to ensure no excessive trapping occurred and avoiding areas with known sensitive habitats or protected species.

Our sampling protocol involved collecting samples every 15 days from each hair snare for a period of three months. This frequency allowed us to obtain sufficient genetic material while minimizing disturbance to the targeted populations. The quantity of faecal matter collected varied depending on the species, ranging from approximately 0.5g to 2g per sample.
--------------------------------------------------
