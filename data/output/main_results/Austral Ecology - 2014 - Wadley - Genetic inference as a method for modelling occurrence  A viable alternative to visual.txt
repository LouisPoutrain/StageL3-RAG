In this study, we employed non-invasive DNA sampling methods to minimize disturbance to wild animal populations. We targeted fecal samples as the primary source of genetic material due to their abundant availability and ease of collection. To collect these samples, we used a combination of visual encounter surveys and camera trapping to locate suitable sampling sites. Once a fresh fecal sample was identified, it was collected using sterile gloves and placed in individual Whirl-Pak bags (Nasco, Fort Atkinson, WI) containing silica beads to preserve DNA integrity. Samples were collected opportunistically during routine field surveys, with no set frequency or quantity limits, but aiming for at least one sample per individual when possible. To minimize disturbance, we ensured that animals were not approached directly and that sampling occurred outside of critical breeding or mating seasons. This approach adheres to Taberlet's definition of non-invasive sampling, as it does not cause direct harm or stress to the animals.

Document hypothétique 2:
--------------------------------------------------
In this study, we employed non-invasive genetic sampling methods to collect DNA from wild animal populations. We focused on collecting feces samples, as they are abundant and contain sufficient amounts of cell-free DNA for genetic analyses (Piggott et al., 2015). To obtain these samples, we used camera traps baited with peanut butter and oats, which were placed along trails or near known animal activity sites. The cameras were left in place for a minimum of seven days to ensure sufficient sampling opportunities. Upon retrieval, the SD cards from the cameras were removed and stored at room temperature until extraction. Prior to deployment, we ensured that all equipment was cleaned with DNA-free wipes to minimize cross-contamination between samples. To minimize disturbance to the animals and their habitats, we avoided placing camera traps near active dens or nests, and ensured that they were not visible from common animal paths or areas of high activity. Additionally, we obtained permits for all sampling activities and adhered strictly to ethical guidelines for wildlife research in Australia (Australian code for the care and use of animals for scientific purposes). This non-invasive approach allowed us to collect genetic material without directly handling or disturbing the animals, aligning with Taberlet's definition of low-impact sampling methods.
--------------------------------------------------

Document hypothétique 3:
--------------------------------------------------
The sampling method employed in this study involved the collection of non-invasive DNA samples from hair snares. The hair snares were constructed using barbed wire covered with a non-toxic, fast-drying adhesive (TrapCo). These snares were placed along known animal trails or at active den sites, and left undisturbed for a period of two weeks before being checked. Upon collection, the hairs adhering to the snares were carefully removed using sterile tweezers and stored in individual zip-top plastic bags containing silica gel desiccant packets until further processing. This passive sampling method was chosen as it minimizes disturbance to the animals and reduces the risk of injury, thus aligning with Taberlet's definition of low-invasive sampling. Furthermore, this method allows for repeated sampling from the same individuals over time without causing harm or stress.
--------------------------------------------------
