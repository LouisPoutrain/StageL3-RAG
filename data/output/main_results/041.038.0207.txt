During genetic analysis of wild animal populations, non-invasive sampling methods are often preferred to minimize disturbance and stress. One such method is the collection of fecal samples from carnivores like wolves (Canis lupus). Fecal samples contain sufficient DNA for genetic analysis, including microsatellite markers, allowing for individual identification and kinship determination.

The protocol used in our study involved the systematic search for wolf scats along trails or near dens during regular surveys. Once located, scats were collected using sterile gloves to avoid contamination. A total of 10 scats were collected per pack, with a minimum interval of two weeks between collections from the same pack to minimize sampling bias due to repeated collection from the same individuals.

To minimize disturbance to the animals and their habitat, no baits or traps were used. Instead, scats were opportunistically collected along survey routes, ensuring minimal impact on animal behavior and ecology. Additionally, care was taken not to disturb or approach dens during collections to avoid causing stress to the animals. This passive sampling method aligns with Taberlet's definition of non-invasive sampling, aiming to minimize disturbance and stress to the target species.

The genetic analysis of wild animals often requires non-invasive sampling methods to minimize disturbance and stress on the target species. One such method employed in this study was the collection of fecal samples from the endangered Amur tiger (Panthera tigris altaica). Fecal samples were collected passively using rectangular traps baited with chicken carcasses, which were set up along known animal trails within the study area. The traps were checked daily for signs of activity and any present feces were collected using sterile gloves and placed in individual Whirl-Pak bags containing silica gel packets to preserve DNA integrity. A total of 10 samples were collected from each tiger over a period of three months, with an average interval of approximately two weeks between collections to minimize disturbance. This passive sampling method allowed for the collection of genetic material without direct contact with the animals, thus reducing potential stress and impact on their wellbeing according to Taberlet's definition of non-invasiveness.
