Document hypothétique 1:
--------------------------------------------------
For non-invasive genetic sampling of gray wolves (Canis lupus), we collected DNA from their scat using a protocol designed to minimize disturbance and potential stress to the animals. We employed hair snares baited with chicken backs at known wolf travel routes and denning areas, following methods described by Murphy et al. (2007). The hair snares were checked daily, and captured hairs were stored in individual Whirl-Pak bags containing a silica desiccant packet to preserve DNA integrity. To minimize the impact on wolf populations, we limited sampling frequency to once every two weeks at each location, ensuring sufficient time for regrowth of lost hairs and minimizing the potential for detection by other wolves. Additionally, we used non-toxic paint to mark trees near hair snares to avoid over-trapping in the same area. Ethical considerations included avoiding locations with signs of recent wolf activity to minimize disturbance and reducing the number of hairs sampled from each individual to prevent excessive hair loss. This approach allowed us to collect genetic material while minimizing potential impacts on wolf populations, adhering to Taberlet et al.'s (1997) definition of non-invasive sampling.
--------------------------------------------------

Document hypothétique 2:
--------------------------------------------------
In this study, non-invasive genetic sampling was employed to collect DNA from gray wolves (Canis lupus) in the northern Rocky Mountains. The primary sampling method involved hair snares, which are considered minimally invasive according to Taberlet's definition. These were constructed using barbed wire attached to trees at wolf-height and baited with elk (Cervus elaphus) carcasses or other attractants. Hair was collected from wolves as they investigated the baits, ensuring no physical contact with the animals. The hair snares were checked daily during the winter months when wolf activity is peak, with all captured hairs being carefully removed and stored in individual envelopes labeled with the location and date of collection.

In addition to hair snares, scat was collected as an alternative non-invasive source of DNA. Scats were located using tracks leading to and from known wolf rendezvous sites or travel corridors. Once found, each scat was carefully collected using gloved hands and placed in a zip-top bag with the location and date recorded. To minimize disturbance, only one scat per individual wolf was collected, even if multiple were present at a site.

Throughout the sampling process, precautions were taken to minimize stress and perturbation to the wolves. Sampling sites were chosen to avoid sensitive areas such as dens or rendezvous sites during denning season. Additionally, hair snares were checked infrequently and removed once a sufficient amount of hair had been collected to obtain a reliable genetic sample. This approach helped maintain the balance between obtaining valuable genetic data while minimizing disturbance to the wild wolf populations.
--------------------------------------------------

Document hypothétique 3:
--------------------------------------------------
For non-invasive genetic sampling of wild animals, we focused on collecting feces as the primary sample type. This method allows us to minimize disturbance and stress to the animals while obtaining sufficient DNA for analysis. We employed a passive collection approach using baited stations consisting of a wooden platform (30 cm x 45 cm) placed approximately 1 meter above the ground, supported by two trees or stakes. The platforms were baited with fresh roadkill or chicken carcasses to attract canids. Feces were collected from these platforms using sterile gloves and forceps, and stored in individual Whirl-Pak bags containing a small amount of silica desiccant. Samples were collected once every 7 days for a period of two months to maximize the chances of obtaining multiple samples per individual while minimizing the risk of cross-contamination between samples. All handling procedures were conducted following approved ethical guidelines to minimize animal disturbance and maintain their natural behavior.
