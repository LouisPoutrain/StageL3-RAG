"DNA sampling methods used in the genetic analysis of wild animals are crucial for obtaining high-quality data while minimizing animal disturbance. In this study, non-invasive methods were employed to collect animal samples from a remote population of wolves (Canis lupus). The primary method involved collecting fecal samples using barbed wire fences coated with petroleum jelly as traps along known wolf territories. These traps were checked daily and any collected scats were stored in individual sterile bags and frozen immediately on site to preserve DNA integrity. Additionally, hair snares baited with scent-laden cotton pads were deployed to collect shed hairs from the animals' pelage. These snares were checked weekly and any collected hairs were stored dry in paper envelopes until further processing. Both methods allowed for passive collection without direct contact with the animals, thus minimizing stress and disturbance. However, these methods require careful consideration of ethical implications regarding animal welfare and potential impacts on wildlife behavior."

Document hypothétique 3:
--------------------------------------------------
To collect non-invasive genetic samples from wild animals, we employed hair snares baited with peanut butter and rolled oats. These passive sampling devices were placed along game trails and at known wildlife crossing points in the study area. The hair snares consisted of barbless hooks attached to monofilament fishing line, which was anchored to a stake driven into the ground. Hair samples were collected from animals as they rubbed against the baited hooks, leaving behind hair strands with follicles attached. No direct contact or manipulation of the animals was required, minimizing disturbance and potential stress. The hair samples were stored in individual zip-top bags and labeled with the location and date of collection. This method allowed us to obtain genetic material from a diverse range of species without causing harm or perturbation to the wildlife population, aligning with the non-invasive sampling definition proposed by Taberlet et al. (2018).
