Document hypothétique 1:
--------------------------------------------------
For this study, we employed a noninvasive DNA sampling method using hair snares to collect genetic material from Mexican wolves (Canis lupus baileyi). This approach minimized animal contact and potential disturbance. Hair snares consisted of barbed wire loops attached to trees or bushes along known wolf travel routes. These devices were checked daily for three days each month during the wolf's active season, from October to May. Upon capture, hair samples were carefully removed using sterile forceps and placed in individual plastic bags. A total of 50 hair snares were deployed across six different pack territories, yielding sufficient DNA for genetic analysis in approximately 70% of sampled individuals. To minimize stress and disturbance, snares were set at heights accessible to wolves but not to other large carnivores or livestock. Additionally, snares were checked frequently to reduce the duration of animal capture. This noninvasive sampling method allowed us to estimate wolf abundance without causing significant perturbation to the wildlife population, aligning with Taberlet's definition of low-invasive sampling methods.
--------------------------------------------------

This approach minimizes disturbance to the animals and reduces the risk of stress or injury. --------------------------------------------------
The DNA sampling methods employed in this study were designed to minimize disturbance and potential harm to the target species, the Mexican wolf (Canis lupus baileyi). A noninvasive approach using hair snares was utilized for collecting genetic material. This method involved placing barbed wire snares baited with a scent lure at strategic locations along known or suspected Mexican wolf travel routes. Hair samples were collected from animals as they passed through the snares, without physical contact between researcher and animal. The snares were checked regularly, and any captured hairs were carefully removed using sterile forceps and placed in labeled ziplock bags for storage. This protocol was approved by the appropriate ethics committee and followed guidelines to minimize stress and potential injury to the animals.
