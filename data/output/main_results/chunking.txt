The equipment used consisted of barbless wire snares attached to trees or other stable structures along animal trails. These snares were baited with appropriate food items specific to the target species and equipped with a timer to record capture events.

Hair samples were collected at regular intervals, with each snare checked weekly for new hair samples. Upon detection of a captured individual, the snare was opened carefully to avoid damaging the hair sample, which was then placed in a sterile collection tube and stored at -20°C until further processing. This protocol allowed us to minimize animal stress and perturbation while ensuring the collection of sufficient genetic material for analysis.

Additionally, we employed a second method involving the collection of fecal samples using non-toxic paint traps. These traps consisted of a bucket filled with water and mixed with non-toxic paint, which adheres to the animal's feet when they step into the trap to drink or investigate. Fecal samples were collected from the surrounding area after each capture event, placed in sterile collection tubes, and stored at -20°C until further processing.

Both sampling methods allowed us to collect DNA samples from wild animals without direct contact or manipulation, thereby minimizing potential stress and disturbance to the targeted species. These methods are considered non-invasive according to Taberlet's definition, as they do not cause tissue damage or require animal handling.
