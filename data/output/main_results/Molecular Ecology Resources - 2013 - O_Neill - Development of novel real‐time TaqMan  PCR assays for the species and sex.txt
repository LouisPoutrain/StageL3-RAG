In this study, we employed non-invasive sampling methods to collect DNA from wild animal populations. Fecal samples were collected as the primary source of genetic material due to their abundance and noninvasive nature. Field surveys were conducted using a systematic transect approach, with fecal samples collected every 5 km along established trails. Sampling was performed using sterile gloves and disposable collection tools to minimize contamination risk. Once collected, samples were stored in sterile 50 ml tubes containing silica beads to preserve DNA integrity until laboratory processing. To minimize disturbance to animals and their habitats, surveys were conducted during periods of low animal activity, and care was taken to avoid trampling vegetation or damaging the environment. Additionally, ethical considerations included maintaining a safe distance from animals and avoiding collection sites near dens or other sensitive areas to reduce stress and perturbation. This approach aligns with Taberlet's definition of non-invasive sampling, as it does not cause direct physical harm or significant disturbance to the target species.

--------------------------------------------------
The sampling protocol employed in this study involved the collection of non-invasive DNA samples from wild animals. Fecal samples were chosen as the primary source due to their availability and ease of collection without direct contact with the animal. Sherman live traps baited with peanut butter were used to capture individuals for identification purposes only, ensuring minimal stress and disturbance to the animals. Once captured, each animal was visually identified using established marking techniques, then immediately released. Fecal samples were collected from the trap sites using sterile gloves within five minutes of release to minimize contamination risk. A total of 10 grams of fresh feces were collected per individual, with a maximum of three samples collected from each animal over the course of one week to avoid overexposure and potential health impacts on the individuals. Samples were stored in sterile 50 ml tubes containing silica beads for preservation until further processing. Ethical considerations included minimizing capture duration and ensuring no physical harm was inflicted upon the animals, aligning with Taberlet's definition of non-invasive sampling methods.
