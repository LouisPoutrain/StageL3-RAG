Methods:
In this study, we used non-invasive genetic sampling techniques to collect DNA from wild animals. We focused on fecal samples as they provide a reliable source of genomic material without causing harm or stress to the animals (Taberlet et al., 1999). Fecal collections were carried out using barcoded scrape-rollers, which minimize contamination between samples and allow for efficient collection in various habitats. These tools were gently rolled over animal feces to collect sufficient genetic material while minimizing disturbance to the habitat. Samples were collected weekly during peak activity periods of each species under investigation to maximize yield and represent different individuals within the population.

To ensure ethical considerations and minimize impact, we followed guidelines for non-invasive sampling protocols (IUCN, 2013). We maintained a distance of at least 50 meters from animals while collecting samples and avoided areas where animals were actively feeding or resting. Samples were stored in sterilized plastic bags at room temperature until extraction. All collected data were used solely for scientific purposes, and no personal identifiers linked to the sampled individuals were recorded.

References:
Taberlet, P., Fumagalli, L., & Broquet, T. (1999). Non‐invasive genetic sampling of wild animal populations: applications to conservation issues. Molecular Ecology, 8(6), 753-762.
IUCN. (2013). Guidelines for Using Non-Invasive Methods in Wildlife Research and Conservation.

Document hypothétique 3:
--------------------------------------------------
Methods:

    Blood samples were collected from wild chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes) using the mark-resight technique to minimize animal handling and stress. Captures were conducted by experienced field assistants who immobilized the animals with a dart gun containing ketamine, a sedative that has minimal effects on cardiovascular function and does not cause respiratory depression. Once immobilized, an approximate 1 ml blood sample was taken from the femoral vein using a heparinized syringe. The wound was then closed with a sterile gauze patch to prevent infection. Samples were stored in liquid nitrogen within 2 hours of collection.

    In addition to active sampling, passive sampling methods were employed by placing hair snare traps along established trails and near sleeping sites. These traps consisted of a loop of monofilament fishing line attached to a tree or other stable object. As the animal passed through the trap, its fur became caught in the line, allowing for the collection of shed hairs without direct contact with the animal.

    To minimize disturbance and potential harm to the animals, traps were checked daily and any captured individuals were released immediately upon discovery. Furthermore, all trapping sites were carefully selected to avoid areas where chimpanzees might be disturbed or stressed by human presence.

    Both blood and hair samples were collected opportunistically during encounters with wild chimpanzees, with no set frequency or duration of sampling. However, efforts were made to collect multiple samples from each individual over time to maximize genetic information and account for potential fluctuations in gene expression due to factors such as seasonality and social status.

    Ethical considerations included obtaining permission from relevant authorities and ensuring that all procedures adhered to established guidelines for the treatment of animals in research. Animal welfare was monitored throughout the study, and any signs of distress or ill health were recorded and addressed promptly by trained field staff.
