These were placed along established trails and at known active sites of the animals. The snares consisted of monofilament fishing line attached to a small piece of plastic tubing containing a barbed wire loop. We checked these snares every two weeks and collected any trapped hairs using sterile forceps. To minimize disturbance, we wore gloves while handling the snares and avoided approaching them during peak activity hours of the pikas. Furthermore, some species lack external genitalia resulting in difficult live-sexing (Williams et al. 2004). Due to such barriers facing the study of elusive mammals, an alternative strategy is to combine non-invasive sampling and genetic approaches. This method facilitates increased sample sizes (Banks et al. 2003) and associated inferences of population history (Morin et al. 1993) and sex-specific patterns (Zgurski and Hik 2012), while minimizing impact on the sampled individual (Taberlet and Luikart 1999). American pika (Ochotona princeps) are small, elusive mammals of conservation concern due to the threat of contemporary climate change (Smith et al. 2004). Applying non-invasive sampling and genetic approaches to the study of pikas is of great importance, as they are difficult to live trap, have poorly distinguished genitalia (Duke 1951), and are easily stressed (MacArthur and Wang 1974;Smith 1974). Previous work tested a polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-restriction fragment length polymorphism analysis of the sex-linked zinc finger gene (ZFX/ZFY) modified from Fontanesi et al. Here, we developed a molecular sexing protocol designed to reliably identify sex in American pika using non-invasively collected hair samples. Our method uses a multiplex PCR to co-amplify fragments of the sex-determining gene Y (SRY; *117 bp, malespecific) and a higher molecular weight (179-203 bp), species-specific autosomal microsatellite [Ocp10; Peacock et al., unpublished (Genbank Acc. No. GQ461705)], using a multi-tube approach (Taberlet et al. 1996). Molecular sexing using the SRY fragment has been previously employed in mammals (Palsbøll and Vader 1992;Vanpe ´et al. 2013); however, to our knowledge this method has not been applied to American pika. Our assay was designed based on the following parameters: (1) a small fragment size for SRY to enable amplification from degraded DNA; (2) a larger autosomal, species-specific fragment to act as a positive PCR control; and (3) sufficient size differential to enable agarose gel electrophoretic detection of multiple bands. DNA extraction protocols for liver (n = 7) and hair (n = 177) samples followed Henry and Russello (2011) (see below for sample details). PCRs were carried out on an ABI Veriti thermal cycler in 12.5 lL reactions containing: 5-10 ng DNA, 10 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl 2 , 0.8 lM of each SRY primer (Table 1), 0.8 lM of each microsatellite primer (Table 1), 200 lM dNTPs, 10 lg bovine serum albumin, and 1U AmpliTaq Gold DNA polymerase (ABI). PCR products were run on a 1.5 % agarose gel containing 2.5 % SYBR Safe (Invitrogen). Samples with the Ocp10 and SRY fragments present were males, and samples with only the Ocp10 were females (Fig. 1). This protocol was validated using samples from individuals of known sex and multiple starting materials (eight hair, seven liver), all of which were accurately diagnosed using our method. The protocol was subsequently applied to 157 pika hair samples collected from a population in Oregon, USA that were previously identified as unique individuals of unknown sex through microsatellite genotyping (Robson 2013). The assay was repeated twice for each sample; in cases of incongruence, a third replicate was performed and a majority rule was applied.
