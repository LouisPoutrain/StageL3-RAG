The DNA sampling methods employed in this study aimed to minimize disturbance and stress to the target wildlife populations. Blood samples were collected from free-ranging animals using a passive capture technique involving Clover traps baited with fresh meat. Upon capture, animals were manually restrained by experienced researchers wearing gloves to avoid contamination. A 2 ml blood sample was taken from the jugular vein using a sterile syringe fitted with a 21-gauge needle. To minimize stress and potential injuries, sampling duration did not exceed five minutes per animal. Blood samples were stored in EDTA tubes at -20°C until DNA extraction. For non-target species encountered during trapping sessions, a passive hair-snare method was used to collect shed hair samples for genetic analysis. Hair snares consisted of barbless wire strands attached to trees along animal trails. Collected hair samples were placed in paper envelopes and stored at room temperature until processing. Both sampling methods complied with ethical guidelines aimed at minimizing disturbance, stress, and potential harm to the animals, adhering to Taberlet's definition of non-invasive sampling.

In this study, we employed non-invasive sampling methods to collect DNA from wild animal populations. For carnivores such as wolves and lynx, we utilized hair snares baited with valerian roots, which were checked daily for trapped individuals. Once an animal was caught, it was quickly released without direct contact or handling. The hairs collected in the snares were stored in individual zip-top bags, labeled with species, date, location, and unique identifier numbers.

For herbivores like deer and roe deer, we used velvet sampling kits to collect shed antlers, which were cleaned and dried before storage in paper bags. These samples were collected opportunistically during field surveys throughout the year.

Fecal samples were also collected from various species across different habitats. We used latex gloves and sterile collection bags to minimize contamination. Samples were stored on ice packs in coolers until transportation to the laboratory for storage at -20°C.

Throughout the sampling process, we took several considerations to minimize stress and disturbance to the animals. Capture durations were kept as short as possible for hair snares, and no direct handling of animals occurred during the sampling process. Additionally, we avoided sampling during sensitive periods such as mating or birthing seasons.
