Methods:
The study employed non-invasive sampling techniques to collect arthropod DNA from households across the United States. Fecal samples were collected using a modification of the protocol described by Murphy et al. (2010). Each household was visited once, and fecal material was collected from floors, carpets, and other accessible surfaces using sterile cotton swabs moistened with 70% ethanol. The swabs were then placed in individual tubes containing 95% ethanol for storage until DNA extraction.

To minimize disturbance to the animals, no trapping or manipulation of the arthropods was performed. Instead, fecal samples were collected passively during short visits to each household. Care was taken to avoid collecting human or pet feces by targeting areas where arthropod activity was evident but not frequented by humans or pets. Additionally, all samples were collected between 10 pm and 6 am to minimize disturbance to the animals and residents.

For DNA extraction, a modified CTAB (hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide) method was used, as described by Aljanabi & Martinez (1997). The quality and quantity of extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis.
--------------------------------------------------

Document hypothétique 2:
--------------------------------------------------
In this study, we employed non-invasive methods for DNA sampling to minimize disturbance to wild animals. We focused on collecting fecal samples due to their availability and the fact that they contain sufficient genetic material for analysis. Fecal samples were collected using sterile gloves directly from the ground in areas where animal activity was evident. To ensure the safety of both researchers and animals, we wore appropriate protective clothing and followed established guidelines for handling biological samples.

The sampling protocol consisted of collecting fecal samples once a week over a period of four months. The frequency of collection was chosen to maximize the chances of obtaining fresh samples while minimizing the risk of repeated sampling from the same individual. A total of 50 samples were collected per site, with a minimum distance of 10 meters between each sample to reduce the likelihood of collecting from the same animal.

To maintain the quality and integrity of the DNA extracted from fecal samples, we used sterile tubes containing preservation buffer (Longmire et al., 2012). Tubes were stored on ice during collection and then transferred to a cooler with ice packs for transportation to the laboratory. Upon arrival at the laboratory, samples were stored at -20°C until further processing.

To minimize the impact of our sampling methods on wild animals, we carefully selected sampling sites away from known dens or resting areas. Additionally, we did not collect samples from areas where animal activity was high during peak activity periods to avoid disturbing animals. By following these considerations and using non-invasive DNA sampling methods, we aimed to minimize stress and perturbation to the wildlife while obtaining valuable genetic data for our study.
--------------------------------------------------

Document hypothétique 3:
--------------------------------------------------
**Methods:**

Non-invasive DNA sampling methods were employed to minimize disturbance and stress to the target wildlife populations. Fecal samples were collected as the primary source of DNA, given their non-invasiveness and rich genetic information (Pavlů et al., 2013). Fresh scats were located by direct observation or by tracking animal movement patterns. A total of 50 fecal samples per species were collected over a three-month period, with an average interval of two weeks between collections from the same individual to avoid pseudoreplication (Waits & Paetkau, 2005). Samples were stored in sterile Whirl-Pak bags containing silica beads to preserve DNA integrity.
