DNA sampling methods used in wildlife genetic analysis can vary greatly depending on the target species and its availability. This study employed a non-invasive approach using owl pellets as a source of small mammal DNA. Owl pellets are regurgitated masses containing undigested remains of prey, including fur, bones, and occasionally, soft tissues.

The collection of owl pellets involved regular field surveys in a specific area known to harbor target species. No handling or disturbance of the animals was necessary, minimizing potential stress and ensuring ethical considerations were met. Pellets were collected using sterile gloves and placed in individual zip-top plastic bags, labeled with date and location of collection. Sampling was conducted monthly over a period of one year to ensure sufficient genetic material was obtained.

Total genomic DNA was extracted from each pellet using a standard phenol-chloroform method (Sambrook et al., 1989). The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, PCR amplification of targeted genetic loci was performed using specific primers for small mammals.

This non-invasive approach allowed for the collection of valuable genetic data without direct contact with the animals, thus minimizing potential disturbance and respecting ethical guidelines for wildlife research.
--------------------------------------------------

--------------------------------------------------
For the DNA sampling methods employed in our study on small mammals, we focused on the collection of owl pellets. This non-invasive technique allowed us to obtain genetic material without directly handling or capturing the animals. We used binoculars and GPS devices to locate active burrows and potential pellet deposition sites. Once located, pellets were collected using sterile tweezers and stored in individual zip-top bags containing silica gel desiccant packs to preserve DNA integrity.

Our sampling protocol involved collecting pellets at intervals of two weeks during the breeding season (April to July) from each of the 20 burrows across five different locations. This frequency was chosen to minimize the impact on the animals' diet and nutritional status, as well as to reduce the risk of collecting related pellets from the same individual over time.

To ensure ethical considerations, all collection sites were accessed with minimal disturbance, and pellets were collected only when fresh ones were available. No animal contact or manipulation occurred during the sampling process, thus minimizing stress and perturbation to the small mammal populations.
--------------------------------------------------

Document hypothétique 3:
--------------------------------------------------
In this study, we employed non-invasive DNA sampling methods to minimize disturbance to the small mammals studied. We collected owl pellets as our primary source of genetic material. Owl pellets are regurgitated masses containing indigestible remains such as hair, bones, and feathers from the prey consumed by owls. These pellets were collected from various locations under the roosts of the Eurasian eagle-owl (Bubo bubo) in Portugal.

The collection of owl pellets is considered a low-invasive sampling method according to Taberlet's definition, as it does not require direct contact with the animals and has minimal impact on their habitats. We collected pellets at monthly intervals over a period of one year to ensure sufficient genetic material was obtained while minimizing disturbance to the owls.

Upon collection, pellets were stored individually in plastic bags and frozen at -20°C within 48 hours to preserve DNA integrity. Prior to DNA extraction, pellets were thawed and visually inspected under bright light to identify small mammal remains. Once identified, these remains were carefully extracted using forceps and tweezers, ensuring no damage was caused to the delicate bones and hairs.

This non-invasive sampling method allowed us to obtain genetic material from various small mammal species without causing stress or perturbation to the animals or their habitats. It also provided a valuable opportunity to study the diet of Eurasian eagle-owls and better understand the trophic interactions within the ecosystem.
