In our genetic sampling protocol, we collected non-invasive fecal samples from wild animals to minimize disturbance and potential harm. We used baited hair snares specifically designed for the targeted species, which were placed along animal trails and checked daily. Upon finding scat within the snares, we carefully removed the sample using sterile gloves and forceps, taking care not to disturb the surrounding environment. Samples were then stored in sterile tubes at -20°C until extraction of genomic DNA. This non-invasive method allows for repeated sampling from individual animals without causing stress or harm, aligning with Taberlet's definition of low-invasiveness as it minimizes direct contact and disturbance.

Document hypothétique 2:
--------------------------------------------------
In this study, we employed non-invasive genetic sampling methods to minimize disturbance and stress to wild animal populations. We targeted fecal samples as they are abundant, easy to collect, and contain sufficient DNA for genetic analysis (Puechmaille et al., 2015). Fecal samples were collected along established trails and other areas of high animal activity using sterile gloves and plastic bags. To maximize the chances of collecting fresh samples, we visited each site daily for a period of five days. We stored the collected samples at -20°C within two hours of collection to preserve DNA integrity (Kohn & Wayne, 1993). To avoid pseudoreplication, we ensured that fecal samples were at least 50 meters apart and checked for unique genetic profiles using microsatellite markers (Waits et al., 2001). This non-invasive sampling approach allowed us to estimate animal density with minimal impact on the targeted populations.
--------------------------------------------------
