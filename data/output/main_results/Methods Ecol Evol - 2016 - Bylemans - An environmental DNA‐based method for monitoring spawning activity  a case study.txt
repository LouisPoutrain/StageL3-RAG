Methods:

For wildlife genetic analysis, we employed an environmental DNA (eDNA) approach to minimize animal disturbance. We collected non-invasively shed tissue samples in the form of skin cells from fish mucus on the surface water of their habitats.

We used dip nets with a mesh size of 5 mm and a handle length of approximately 1.2 meters to collect water samples. The nets were submerged briefly at various locations around each sampling site to capture mucus and other particles. A total of five replicate water samples were collected from each site, with each sample consisting of approximately 30 mL of water.

The samples were then immediately filtered using a portable vacuum filtration system connected to a Sterivex-GP filter unit (0.22 µm pore size) to capture the eDNA. The filters were subsequently placed in sterile tubes and stored at -20°C until further processing.

To minimize the impact on animals, we avoided sampling during periods of high stress or activity, such as spawning seasons or migration periods. Additionally, we followed guidelines for ethical wildlife research practices to ensure minimal disturbance to the animals and their habitats.

This non-invasive eDNA approach allowed us to monitor genetic diversity without capturing or handling the animals, thereby reducing potential stress and minimizing invasiveness according to Taberlet's definition (Taberlet et al., 2018).

Methods Ecol Evol - 2016 - Bylemans - An environmental DNA‐based method for monitoring spawning activity: A case study

In this study, we employed an environmental DNA (eDNA) sampling approach to monitor the spawning activity of brown trout (Salmo trutta). The eDNA was collected from water samples taken from the upstream reaches of streams where spawning occurs. This non‐invasive method involves filtering a known volume of water through a glass fiber filter, which captures the DNA released by the fish into the water. The filters were then stored at −20°C until DNA extraction could be performed. This protocol allows for a high throughput of samples, making it suitable for large‐scale monitoring studies. Furthermore, this method minimizes disturbance to the animals and their habitats, aligning with Taberlet's definition of non‐invasive sampling methods.
--------------------------------------------------

Document hypothétique 3:
--------------------------------------------------
"For the DNA sampling methods used in this study, we employed a non-invasive approach using environmental DNA (eDNA) from water samples. We collected 1L of surface water from each site using sterile bottles, taking care not to disturb the aquatic environment excessively. Samples were collected monthly over a period of six months to monitor temporal changes in spawning activity. To minimize potential impacts on animal behavior and habitat integrity, we avoided sampling during peak spawning periods or in areas with high densities of target species. All samples were stored at 4°C until processing for DNA extraction."
