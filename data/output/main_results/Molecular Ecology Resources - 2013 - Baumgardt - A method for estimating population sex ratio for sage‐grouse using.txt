In this study, we employed a non-invasive DNA sampling method using fecal samples to minimize disturbance and potential harm to the wild sage-grouse population. We collected fecal samples from captive birds housed at a conservation facility using sterile gloves and plastic bags. Fecal collection occurred every other day for two weeks, ensuring adequate time between collections to prevent depletion of nutrients in the diet. A total of 10 grams of feces were collected per sample, placed in individual Whirl-Pak bags, and stored on ice before being transferred to a -80°C freezer within four hours of collection. To minimize stress, all handling was conducted by experienced personnel wearing camouflage clothing to blend with the environment, and birds were allowed to habituate to our presence for one week prior to sampling initiation. This approach aligns with Taberlet's definition of non-invasive sampling, as it avoids direct contact with the animals and does not cause significant perturbation or harm to their habitat.
--------------------------------------------------

Document hypothétique 2:
--------------------------------------------------
For genetic analysis of wild animal populations, non-invasive sampling methods were employed to minimize disturbance and stress to the animals. Fecal samples were collected as they are readily available and provide sufficient DNA for analysis (Piggott et al., 2015). Samplers used glove-compressed DNA swabs to collect the fecal material, ensuring minimal contamination with exogenous DNA. Sampling was conducted along established trails frequented by the target species, with a maximum of five samples collected per trail to avoid over-sampling and potential alteration of behavior. Samples were stored in individual sealed bags containing silica beads to maintain dryness and prevent degradation until they could be transported to the laboratory for processing. To minimize disturbance, sampling was conducted during the early morning hours when the animals are less active. Additionally, all samplers wore gloves and moved silently to avoid disturbing wildlife activity.
--------------------------------------------------

Document hypothétique 3:
--------------------------------------------------
For non-invasive sampling of wild animals, we employed a protocol involving the collection of fecal pellets. This method was chosen as it is considered minimally invasive according to Taberlet's definition, causing no direct disturbance or harm to the animals (Taberlet et al., 1999). We used hair traps baited with peanut butter and oats to collect fecal samples from greater sage-grouse (Centrocercus urophasianus) in Colorado, USA. The traps were checked daily for signs of visitation and any collected pellets were stored in sterile zip-top bags at -20°C until further processing. This approach allowed us to obtain sufficient genetic material while minimizing stress and disturbance to the targeted population.

Abundance of pumas After individualizing puma faecal samples, a capture-recapture history of each animal was established for the 8-month sampling period. To estimate abundance ( N), we opted for an open population model as we assumed that animals could leave or immigrate into the study area during the sampling period. Pumas are vagile and have large home ranges and we had previous information that suggested some may frequently disperse throughout the matrix (Miotto et al., 2012). We estimated N with the Jolly-Seber probability model (Jolly, 1965;Seber, 1965), using the POPAN formulation (Schwarz & Arnason, 1996), implemented in MARK (White & Burnham, 1999). This open population model assumes the existence of a 'superpopulation' (N) in which unmarked animals have the same probability of capture as marked animals in the population (assumption of equal catchability), survival rates are homogeneous for marked and unmarked animals, the sampled area is constant, and there is no loss of mark (genetic tag in our case) during the sampling period. The parameters of this model are p i , the probability of capture of marked and unmarked individuals on occasion i, ф i , the probability of survival of marked and unmarked individuals from occasion i to i + 1, and b i , the probability of a new animal entering the population between occasions i and i + 1. To test the validity of the above assumptions a goodness of fit test was performed using RELEASE v. 3.0 (Burnham et al., 1987) and by accessing the deviance of 1,000 bootstrapped simulations based on the global timedependent Cormack-Jolly-Seber model (Dunstan et al., 2011). We selected between eight models, all nested inside the global model, allowing for temporal variation in all three parameters [ p(t)ф(t)b(t)N(.)]. The tested models are described in Table 1. To guarantee numerical convergence a multinomial link function was used for the b i parameters (constraining a set of real parameters to sum to 1.00), and in the case of the abundance parameter an identity link function was used, permitting the parameter to have values outside the [0, 1] interval. Models were ranked using the Akaike information criterion (AICc) adjusted for small population sizes. The model with the lowest AICc value was considered the model that best balanced bias and precision (White & Burnham, 1999) and, therefore, the most appropriate model for accurate estimation of abundance.
