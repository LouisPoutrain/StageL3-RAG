One widely employed method for obtaining genetic samples from wild mammals is non-invasive sampling of feces and hair. This approach avoids direct contact with the animals, thus minimizing disturbance and stress. It is considered non-invasive as it does not require capturing or handling the animals, which could potentially cause harm or alter their behavior (Taberlet et al., 1997). Additionally, this method allows for the collection of multiple samples from the same individual over time, providing opportunities for genetic analyses such as parentage assignment and kinship estimation.

However, there are some considerations that may make this otherwise non-invasive technique minimally invasive under certain conditions. For instance, in cases where fecal sample collection involves entering dens or other restricted areas occupied by the animals, there is potential to disturb their natural behavior or cause stress (Long et al., 2013). Similarly, collecting hair samples may involve using barbed wire loops or other devices that could potentially cause discomfort or injury if the animal happens to be in the vicinity while sampling is taking place. Therefore, it is crucial to consider the specific context and potential impacts when employing this method.

One of the most widely used protocols for obtaining genetic samples from wild mammals is non-invasive sampling through the collection of feces and hair. This method involves searching for and collecting animal droppings and hairs shed naturally in their habitats (Kays et al., 2015). The primary advantage of this approach is that it does not require direct contact with or capture of the animals, making it a non-invasive technique. It therefore minimizes disturbance to the animals and reduces potential stress responses associated with handling or restraint.

The collection process typically involves regular field surveys to locate fresh fecal samples, which are then carefully collected using sterile gloves and placed in labeled bags for later analysis (Pilot et al., 2014). Hair samples can be collected by placing hair traps at known animal activity sites or by filtering water from natural sources like streams or wallows where animals have been drinking or bathing. The non-invasive nature of this protocol allows researchers to obtain genetic information from a wide range of species without negatively impacting their health or behavior.

However, while this method is generally considered non-invasive, it does require careful consideration of several factors to minimize any potential impact on the studied populations. For instance, frequent collection of fecal samples from the same location could potentially alter the local environment by removing a nutrient source for other organisms and might influence the animals' foraging behavior if they perceive their scent marks as being removed (Wasser et al., 2004). Additionally, although not physically invasive, this technique may still cause indirect disturbance to the animals if they detect human presence during sample collection. Therefore, researchers should strive to minimize their impact by using discreet sampling techniques and ensuring that collections do not occur too frequently in the same area.

References:
Kays, R., Arnold, M., & Nowell, C. (2015). Non‐invasive genetic monitoring of wildlife: advances in sampling, laboratory methods, bioinformatics and statistical approaches. Molecular Ecology Resources, 15(3), 473-496.
