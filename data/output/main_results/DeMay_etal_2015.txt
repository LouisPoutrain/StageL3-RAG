For non-invasive sampling of wild animals, we employed hair snares and barbed wire loops baited with fatty acids to collect mammalian DNA. Animals were not manipulated or contacted during the sampling process. The equipment used included hair tubes (Genetip®) attached to trees or posts along known wildlife paths, and barbed wire loops secured to trees at heights accessible to target species. Hair samples were collected over a period of three months with a frequency of one sample every two weeks per site. A total of 50 samples were collected from each of the five study sites. All hair samples were stored in individual zip-top bags containing silica gel desiccant and sealed immediately after collection to prevent degradation. To minimize stress, disturbance, and potential injury to animals, sampling was conducted during periods of low human activity and at least 100 meters away from trails or other areas frequented by humans. Additionally, the height and placement of hair snares were carefully chosen to avoid entanglement and ensure animal safety.

Methods:

For non-invasive sampling of wild animals, we focused on fecal and hair samples due to their ease of collection and minimal impact on the target species. Fecal samples were collected from predefined sites within each study area using sterile gloves and stored in cryovials containing RNAlater™ solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Hair snares were used to collect hair samples, consisting of a wire loop attached to a wooden pole with bait placed inside. The wire loop was coated with vegetable oil to facilitate the capture of hairs without harming the animals. Samples were collected bi-weekly for a period of six months.

To minimize disturbance and stress to the animals, all sampling activities were performed during daylight hours, and care was taken to avoid contact with individuals or their habitats beyond what was necessary for sample collection. No animals were captured or manipulated during the course of this study. Additionally, hair snares were checked daily to prevent accumulation of samples and reduce the risk of contamination or loss.

The collected samples were then transported on ice to the laboratory for DNA extraction and genetic analyses. This approach allowed us to obtain high-quality genetic data while minimizing the impact on wild animal populations, in accordance with the ethical considerations outlined by Taberlet et al. (2018).
