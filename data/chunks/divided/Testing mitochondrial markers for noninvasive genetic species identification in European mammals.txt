Due to limitation of available DNA, however, three primer pairs were not tested for seven samples (Table 1). We used a standard PCR protocol with 15 ll reaction volumes consisting of 3 ll undiluted DNA template, 0.66 U Mg-free TAQ DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs), 1 9 PCR buffer (New England Biolabs), 3 mM MgCl 2 , and 0.3 lM of each primer. Amplification was performed with 5 min initial denaturation at 94 °C, 40 cycles of 45 s at 94 °C, 30 s at 54 °C, 30 s at 72 °C, and a 10 min final elongation at 72 °C. Purified amplicons were run on a 3730 DNA analyzer (Applied Biosystems). Sequences were processed manually and quality checked using sequence scanner software v1.0 (Applied Biosystems). All sequences where blasted using default settings in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank.
Safe species determination was possible in most cases with readable continuous fragment length (CRL) of[50 bp (Table 1). While all primers generated at least some readable fragments allowing for correct species identification, amplification success and resulting rates of successful species determination differed considerably among markers. Only three samples could be successfully assigned to the correct species with the 658 bp 'barcoding fragment' (COI), indicating its limited suitability for noninvasively collected samples due to its considerable size (Table 1). While the control region is a frequently targeted region in numerous studies on genetic diversity in mammals due to is high mutation rates, the two tested, size variable (*370-800 bp, depending on the respective species) fragments provided intermediate success rates (47 and 62 % for CrL and CRS, respectively). In contrast, use of any of the three remaining fragments (CytB, 12S, and 16S) resulted in high rates of successful species identifications of [80 %. With length of 215 (12S), 234 (CytB) and 244 bp (16S) these fragments are well in the range of markers commonly used for samples of low DNA quality (Taberlet et al. 1999).
To a certain extent, all primers amplified across taxa and no clear trends where observed here based on this rather small sample. However, predominantly carnivorous species showed higher success rates than herbivores when using scats (68 % compared to 48 %, respectively, Table 2), which can be explained by the presence of inhibitory substances in plant tissues. Due to the overall high availability of cytochrome B sequence data in mammals, our tested CytB primer pair might be generally preferred when one has to rely on publicly available sequence database information for species determination. However, we also stress the importance for considering potential limitations of using single mtDNA markers arising from current or past hybridisation, lineage sorting or high intraspecific and cryptic variation.
