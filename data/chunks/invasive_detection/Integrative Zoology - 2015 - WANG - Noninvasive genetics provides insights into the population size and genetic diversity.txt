[SECTION] title
Noninvasive genetics provides insights into the population size and genetic diversity of an Amur tiger population in China

[SECTION] abstract
Understanding population size and genetic diversity is critical for effective conservation of endangered species. The Amur tiger (Panthera tigris altaica) is the largest felid and a flagship species for wildlife conservation. Due to habitat loss and human activities, available habitat and population size are continuously shrinking. However, little is known about the true population size and genetic diversity of wild tiger populations in China. In this study, we collected 55 fecal samples and 1 hair sample to investigate the population size and genetic diversity of wild Amur tigers in Hunchun National Nature Reserve, Jilin Province, China. From the samples, we determined that 23 fecal samples and 1 hair sample were from 7 Amur tigers: 2 males, 4 females and 1 individual of unknown sex. Interestingly, 2 fecal samples that were presumed to be from tigers were from Amur leopards, highlighting the significant advantages of noninvasive genetics over traditional methods in studying rare and elusive animals. Analyses from this sample suggested that the genetic diversity of wild Amur tigers is much lower than that of Bengal tigers, consistent with previous findings. Furthermore, the genetic diversity of this Hunchun population in China was lower than that of the adjoining subpopulation in southwest Primorye Russia, likely due to sampling bias. Considering the small population size and relatively low genetic diversity, it is urgent to protect this endangered local subpopulation in China.

[SECTION] introduction
Accurate estimation of population size is a critical component of conserving endangered species as population size provides a metric for determining the success of conservation strategies, informs the process of allocating resources, and helps determine conservation priorities (Zhan et al. 2006;Wei et al. 2012). However, due to small population sizes and the concerns associated with handling protected species, it is difficult to accurately estimate the population size of endangered species using traditional methods such as line transect surveys (Blanc et al. 2013;Ripple et al. 2014). Successful conservation of endangered species will also conserve genetic diversity, which is associated with evolutionary potential and reflects the capacity of a population to respond to environmental changes (Wei et al. 2012;Zhu et al. 2013). Assessment of genetic diversity has, therefore, become an important component of assessing the status of wildlife populations.
The tiger (Panthera tigris Linnaeus, 1758) is one of the largest felid species and a flagship species for wildlife conservation (Luo et al. 2004). It is a top predator, which makes it ecologically important in natural systems. However, due to anthropogenic habitat loss, poaching and disease, tiger populations have decreased sharply in recent decades (Gilbert et al. 2015;Robinson et al. 2015). Historically, there were 9 subspecies of tigers in the world, but 4 of them have become extinct in the wild in the twentieth century (Luo et al. 2004;Tilson et al. 2004).
The Amur tiger (Panthera tigris altaica Temminck, 1844), one of the largest subspecies of tiger, mainly lives in Northeast China and the Far East of Russia, and possibly in North Korea (Russello et al. 2004). With less than 600 individuals alive in the wild, the Amur tiger is classified as Endangered on the IUCN Red List (Miquelle et al. 2011) and is a category I species under the wild animal protection law in China. In 2000 it was estimated that approximately 20 tigers live in northeast China (Yu et al. 2000;Li et al. 2001), and probably none in North Korea. No more than 500 tigers habituate the Far East of Russia (Miquelle et al. 2010), and the population is divided into 2 subpopulations, with the larger subpopulation located in the Sikhote-Alin Mountains and the smaller subpopulation in southwest Primorye (Henry et al. 2009;Sorokin et al. 2016).
Due to the low population density, along with its nocturnal, elusive behavior, it is difficult to assess the size and genetic diversity of wild Amur tiger populations. Traditional survey methods using footprints, marking sites and urine cannot be used to accurately estimate population size (Ale et al. 2007). The advent of noninvasive genetic sampling has greatly facilitated accurate population censuses of wildlife (Wei et al. 2012). This noninvasive method extracts genetic information from materials such as feces, hair, feathers or urine, without disturbing targeted animals. The noninvasive genetic data can be used to obtain not only basic sample information such as species identity, individual identity and sex (Zhan et al. 2006;Guo et al. 2011), but also important population genetic parameters such as genetic diversity, genetic structure and demographic history (Zhang et al. 2007;Hu et al. 2011).
In this study, we used noninvasive genetic sampling to estimate the population size and genetic diversity of wild Amur tigers in Jilin, China. We also discuss the implications of our findings for the conservation of this flagship species.

[SECTION] sample collection and dna extraction
We searched for Amur tiger feces and hair samples along regular monitoring transect lines in Hunchun National Nature Reserve, Jilin Province, China, during October 2010-March 2015 (Fig. 1). We collected fecal samples that we inferred to be from wild Amur tigers and that were as fresh as possible based on the status of the mucosal outer layer of the feces. In total, 55 fecal samples and 1 hair sample were collected and preserved in -20 °C refrigerator until DNA extraction.
As a pretreatment before the extraction of DNA, each fecal sample was immersed in 99% ethanol to collect cells sloughed from the host animal's intestine. The mixture of feces and ethanol was vortexed and then this solution was transferred into a new centrifuge tube. Next, the mixed solution was centrifuged (1700 g, 10 min) to precipitate the sloughed intestinal cells and the supernatant was discarded (Zhang et al. 2006). The preceding steps were repeated 2 or 3 times and, finally, the deposit was dried to eliminate the ethanol. After pretreatment, fecal DNA was extracted with the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Hair DNA was extracted using proteinase K digestion in a PCR-compatible buffer (Allen et al. 1998).

[SECTION] species identification
To identify whether the noninvasive samples were from a tiger, we designed a pair of universal primers using the mitochondrial COI gene sequence (Pan-COI-F: 5'-TCAGTCTCYTAATYCGAGCCGA-3'; Pan-COI-R: 5'-AAYGGRACYARYCAGTTTCC-3'). This pair of universal primers amplifies a 157-bp sequence, which is suitable for species identification within the genus Panthera and the amplification of noninvasive genetic samples. The PCR amplification was carried out in a 30-μL reaction containing 15 μL HotStar-Taq Master Mix (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), 0.1 μg/μL BSA, 0.25 μM forward primer, 0.25 μM reverse primer and 3 μL of DNA extract. The PCR cycling conditions included an initial denaturation (15 min at 95 °C) and a touchdown PCR (a total of 38 cycles of 30 s at 94 °C, 40 s at T anneal , 50 s at 72 °C), followed by a final extension (10 min at 72 °C). T anneal was decreased by 2 °C every 4 cycles from 54 °C to 50 °C, which was used for the following 30 cycles. The mixture of 3 μL PCR product and 2 μL loading buffer was electrophoresized on 2% agarose gel. Positive PCR products were Sanger-sequenced bidirectionally on an ABI3730xl sequencer. The DNA sequences obtained were assembled and compared against the GenBank database with BLASTN to identify the species source of the samples.

[SECTION] sex identification
For sex identification, we used 2 pairs of primers (ZFX-PF/ZFX-PR, DBY7-PF/DBY7-PR) to amplify the ZFX gene of the X chromosome and the DBY7 gene of the Y chromosome, whose PCR fragment sizes were 205 and 156 bp, respectively (Sugimoto et al. 2006). This amplification was carried out in a 10-μL reaction containing 5 μL HotStar-Taq Master Mix, 0.1 μg/μL BSA, 0.25 μM each ZFX primer, 0.3 μM each DBY7 primer and 1 μL of the DNA extract. The PCR cycling conditions included an initial denaturation (15 min at 95 °C) and a touchdown PCR (a total of 40 cycles of 15 s at 94 °C, 30 s at T anneal , 45 s at 72 °C), followed by a final extension (10 min at 72 °C). T anneal was decreased by 2 °C every 2 cycles from 60°C to 50 °C, which was used for the following 30 cycles. The mixture of 3 μL PCR and 2 μL loading buffer was electrophoresized on 2% agarose gel. Three PCR repeats were simultaneously performed for each DNA extract. When both ZFX and DBY7 gene fragments were amplified successfully twice or more, the sample was determined to be male, whereas when only ZFX fragment was amplified successfully 3 times, the sample was determined to be female. Otherwise, additional PCR repeats were performed. Finally, the sample that could not meet the identification criteria was recorded as of unknown sex. Blood DNA of a male and a female tiger from zoos was used as positive controls and a PCR reaction without DNA was used as a negative control.

[SECTION] microsatellite genotyping and individual identification
We originally chose 14 microsatellite loci for optimal primer selection: Pati 02, Pati 09, Pati 10, Pati 12, Pati 17 and Pati 18 (Wu et al. 2009); E7, 3E6, E21B and D10 (Bhagavatula & Singh 2006);FCA43, FCA441 and FCA304 (Raymond et al.1999); and HDZ170 (Williamson et al. 2002). Based on  . We used a stringent multitube approach to obtain reliable consistent genotypes for the samples collected (Taberlet et al. 1996). First, PCR amplification was performed in triplicate for each locus and we set the criteria that the usable combined genotypes must include at least 7 microsatellite loci. If the same heterozygous genotype was produced at least twice, the locus was regarded as heterozygous and if the same homozygous genotype was produced at least 3 times, the locus was regarded as homozygous. If the genotype could not be determined, additional PCR amplifications were performed. Individual identification was performed for all of the usable genotypes following the method of Zhan et al. (2006). We used Micro-Checker (Van Oosterhout et al. 2004) to assess the presence of null alleles and genotyping errors, such as large allele dropout and stuttering, in the final result of individual identification. To test the discrimination power of the set of microsatellite loci, we computed the probability of pairs of individuals bearing an identical multilocus genotype (P ID ) using GIMLET 1.3.1 (Valière 2002). The more conservative P ID for full-sibs, P ID (sibs), was also estimated as an upper limit to the P ID value.

[SECTION] genetic diversity
We calculated the mean number of alleles per locus (N A ), the expected heterozygosity (H E ) and the observed heterozygosity (H O ), and tested the linkage disequilibrium between pairwise loci using ARLEQUIN 3.5 (Excoffier et al. 2005). Deviations from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium for each loci and the population were assessed using an exact probability test implemented in GENEPOP 4.0 (Rousset 2008). Significance values for multiple comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction (Rice 1989). To assess the level of genetic diversity of the wild Amur tiger population, we compared our results with those of other noninvasive genetics studies of wild tiger populations (Bhagavatula & Singh 2006;Mondol et al. 2009;Borthakur et al. 2011Borthakur et al. , 2013;;Sugimoto et al. 2012;Caragiulo et al. 2015;Sorokin et al. 2016).

[SECTION] results
Of 55 fecal samples and 1 hair sample, we successfully amplified and sequenced the mtDNA COI fragments in 25 fecal samples and 1 hair sample. The failed amplification of 30 fecal samples was most likely due to low quantity and/or quality of fecal DNA. BLAST identified that of 26 noninvasive samples, 23 of 25 fecal samples and 1 hair sample were from Amur tigers; 2 fecal samples were from Amur leopards (Panthera pardus orientalis Schlegel, 1857). These 26 samples, including 2 leopard samples, were further genotyped with 9 microsatellite loci. As a result, 26 samples were genotyped successfully with at least 7 loci. From the 24 tiger samples, we identified 7 unique genotypes, each representing an individual tiger (Fig. 1). Three individuals were sampled 5 times, 2 individuals were sampled 3 times, and the other 2 individuals were sampled twice and once, respectively. 6 of 7 tigers were sampled during January 2014 to March 2015. Based on our analyses, 2 of these tigers were males, 4 were  females and 1 was of unknown sex. The 2 leopard samples were from 2 individuals: 1 female and the other of unknown sex. Among the microsatellite dataset from the 7 tigers, Micro-Checker analysis detected no null alleles or genotyping errors, suggesting the reliability of our microsatellite genotyping. P ID analysis showed that this set of 9 microsatellite loci had a high individual discrimination power, with a probability of 3.34E-05 for producing an identical multi-locus genotype. Even across the 7 most polymorphic loci there was a P ID value of 1.24E-04 (Fig. 2), which was enough for the study of small populations of wild Amur tigers.
Based on the individual identification and the sampling locations, we estimated the distances between locations of individual tigers. The farthest distance recorded between two locations of one animal was 75.2 km over 41 days for one female and the fastest movement recorded was 10.3 km in 1 day for another female.
Genetic diversity analysis showed that for the Hunchun Amur tiger population, the N A was 2.56, and the mean H E and H O were 0.369 and 0.455, respectively (Table 1). Of 9 loci, only FCA043 deviated from the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (P = 0.049), but this deviation was not significant after we corrected for multiple comparisons. Across the Hunchun tiger population there was no deviation from the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (P > 0.05). There was linkage disequilibrium only for Pati 09 and E7 (P = 0.019), but not after we corrected for multiple comparisons. Compared with other noninvasive studies of wild tigers, the genetic diversity we observed among Amur tigers was much lower than that of the Bengal tiger and lower than that of an Amur tiger population in Russia (Table 2).

[SECTION] discussion
In spite of several decades of continuous protection, the population size and habitat of wild Amur tigers continued to decrease. In China, the population of wild Amur tigers between 1974 and 1976 was estimated to be around 151 (Chen et al. 2007). However, in 2000s only approximately 20 Amur tigers lived in Northeast China (Yu et al. 2000;Li et al. 2001). Specifically, in Jilin Province there were between 7 and 9 tigers and there were 5 to 7 tigers in Heilongjiang Province (Yu et al. 2000;Li et al. 2001). In this study, based on genetic information we identified 7 Amur tigers, a number similar to population estimates using traditional methods. Six of 7 tigers were sampled during January 2014 to March 2015. Even though a 15-month period almost certainly violates the assumption of closure required for rigorous population surveys, our findings suggested that a minimum of 6 individuals have lived in this region. Our samples were collected along regular line transect survey rather than exhaustive sampling; thus, the true population size in this region is most likely to be even higher.
H E and N A are widely-used for comparing genetic diversity in different studies, minimizing the effects of sampling bias and different numbers of markers. We found that the Amur tiger had much lower genetic diversity than the Bengal tiger, which is consistent with previous findings that Amur tigers had the lowest genetic variation among the extant subspecies of tiger (Luo et al. 2008;Alasaad et al. 2011). The very low genetic diversity may result from historical and recent population bottlenecks (Henry et al. 2009;Alasaad et al. 2011). Based on 12 loci genotypes of 95 individuals, Henry et al. (2009) detected a genetic signature of a historical population bottleneck, most likely due to the founder effect of postglacial colonization. Recently, using the 11 loci genotypes of 15 individuals, Alasaad et al. (2011) found a signature of a recent population bottleneck, which was attributed to the well-documented demographic decline of the Amur tiger population in the 1940s (Henry et al. 2009). In contrast, the Bengal tiger generally has much higher genetic diversity (Bhagavatula & Singh 2006;Mondol et al. 2009a;Borthakur et al. 2010Borthakur et al. , 2013;;Sugimoto et al. 2014), which may result from the historically high population size in the Indian subcontinent (Mondol et al. 2009b). With more samples and individuals, our genetic diversity estimates are similar to a very recent study of the Hunchun Amur tiger population (Caragiulo et al. 2015). However the lower genetic diversity of Amur tigers in Hunchun, China compared to adjacent animals in southwest Primorye, Russia (Henry et al. 2009;Sugimoto et al. 2012;Sorokin et al. 2016) can likely be explained by sampling bias. Tiger habitat in Hunchun is connected to southwest Primorye, and therefore animals from the two countries belong to a single population, which is genetically distinct from the largest population of Amur tigers in the Sikhote-Alin Mountains of Russia (Henry et al. 2009;Sorokin et al. 2016). The population size of the southwest Primorye tiger subpopulation was estimated to be 16-21 (Sugimoto et al. 2012) tigers in the Hunchun-southwest Primorye population will require incorporation of data from both of these subpopulations.
Our data on the mtDNA COI sequence identified that 2 fecal samples were from Amur leopards, suggesting that reporting tiger presence based on scat may not represent an accurate approach, as discriminating scats from tigers and leopards is clearly subject to error. Noninvasive genetics may provide significant advantages over traditional methods in species identification, sex identification and population census, although genotyping errors can occur as well, such as allele dropout or false alleles. Interestingly, our 9 microsatellite loci also successfully genotyped fecal samples of Amur leopards, suggesting the suitability of these particular loci for the study of the species. Finally, because the alleles of most loci differed between the tiger and the leopard, these microsatellite loci are also potentially suitable markers for distinguishing between tigers and leopards.
