[SECTION] title
Examining genetic diversity and identifying polymorphic microsatellite markers for noninvasive genetic sampling of the Indochinese leopard (Panthera pardus delacouri)

[SECTION] abstract
Indochinese leopard (Panthera pardus delacouri) is one of the least-known leopard subspecies and occurs in mainland South-east Asia. Given the elusive nature, low density, and large movement ranges of leopard, noninvasive genetic sampling may be an effective approach for studying this subspecies. We examined the potential for 18 microsatellite loci to allow reliable identification of noninvasively collected leopard samples based on feces collected in eastern Cambodia. The expected heterozygosity calculated from 5 to 7 leopard individuals ranged from 0.49 to 0.91 with an average of 0.73, showing a high level of genetic diversity. Only the five most informative loci decreased the probability of identity for siblings (P ID-sib ) to less than 0.01, which would be sufficient for abundance estimation. A further increase of the number of loci up to 8-10 decreased the P ID-sib to the level of 0.001-0.0001, which may be useful in cases of parentage analysis or forensic analysis. The microsatellite markers shown in the present study achieved a sufficient level of accuracy for individual identification, and thus, will be useful for examining abundance, genetic structure, or relatedness of the Indochinese leopard in future.

[SECTION] (no title)
Noninvasive genetic sampling is an alternative approach to camera-trapping for monitoring large carnivores given their inherent low density and large movement ranges (Nowell and Jackson, 1996). For implementation of this approach appropriate genetic markers, which ensure accurate individual identification, are necessary. In this study, we aim to identify useful microsatellite markers for individual identification of noninvasive genetic samples of leopard.
We obtained 83 Indochinese leopard fecal samples from Mondulkiri Protected Forest eastern Cambodia. These were collected during large carnivore surveys in the 2009/2010 dry-season. All fecal samples were preserved in DMSO buffer (Seutin et al., 1991) in the field. Species confirmation, as leopard, of the samples was achieved through sequencing a part of the cytochrome b gene after extracting DNA from feces by the QIAamp DNA stool mini kit (QIA-GEN). We subsequently selected relatively good quality samples out of the 83 samples by conducting sex identification following the method of Sugimoto et al. (2006). Because the sex identification primers were developed for the Far eastern leopard (P. p. orientalis) and Amur tiger (P. tigris altaica), we sequenced several PCR products and confirmed that the primers amplified the target regions in both X and Y chromosomes from our Indochinese leopard samples. Based on the band intensity in the sex identification, we selected six good quality samples. Subsequently, they were genotyped two to three times at the following 38 candidate loci (FCA001, FCA008, FCA026, FCA043, FCA052, FCA075, FCA077, FCA090, FCA094, FCA096, FCA097, FCA098, FCA105, FCA123, FCA126, FCA139, FCA161, FCA205, FCA211, FCA220, FCA224, FCA229, FCA230, FCA232, FCA247, FCA279, FCA304, FCA309, FCA310, FCA391, FCA441, FCA453, FCA506, FCA628, FCA672, FCA678, F41: Menotti- Raymond et al., 1999;6HDZ007: Williamson et al., 2002). The genotyping data on six samples, which we later identified as belonging to four individuals, were evaluated based on the following criteria; amplifying short lengths of DNA, ease of amplification, ease of scoring, and presence of more than two alleles. Then we selected 18 loci out of the 38 as potentially suitable for individual identification. It should be noted, however, that this initial screening was conducted on the basis of only six fecal samples (i.e., four individuals) and a limited number of PCR amplifications without quantifying the genotyping errors. We did this brief screening because it was not possible to scrutinize all 38 loci due to the limited amount of fecal DNA. The additional 20 markers are therefore not necessarily unsuitable for noninvasive genetic sampling, particularly those markers which have been used in studies of leopards elsewhere in the species' range (e.g., Dutta et al., 2012;Mondol et al., 2009;Sugimoto et al., 2014).
To evaluate the genetic variation of the selected 18 loci, we performed genotyping at least three times for the remaining 77 leopard samples (at least two times for the six good quality samples) by using a multiplex PCR with the marker combinations presented in Table 1. A heterozygous genotype was determined when both alleles were detected at least two times, whereas a homozygote was determined when one allele was detected at least three times, among three to seven PCR amplifications. Samples with successful genotyping at less than ten loci were discarded. The genotyping error rate per locus was calculated using Eqs. ( 1) and (3) from Broquet and Petit (2004). The PCR reactions for the multilocus genotyping were all performed in 10 L volumes including 1× PCR buffer, 0.2 mM each dNTP, 3.0 mM MgCl 2 , 0.4 M of each forward and reverse primer (one of them marked with a fluorescent dye), 4 g of BSA, 0.3 units of Taq DNA polymerase (Applied Biosystems), and 1.0 L of DNA extract, and the reaction conditions were those described by Menotti-Raymond et al. (1999), although we increased the number of second cycles from 20 to 30, i.e. 40 cycles in total. The PCR products were analyzed with an ABI 3100 automatic sequencer (Applied Biosystems) and the genotyping data were collected using GeneScan (Applied Biosystems). Genetic diversity statistics were calculated by the program GENEPOP (Raymond and Rousset, 1995) and GIMLET (Valière, 2002). Linkage disequilibrium between all pairs of the 18 loci and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium for each locus were assessed by the program FSTAT (Goudet, 2001), where a sequential Bonferroni correction was used for multiple tests (Rice, 1989).
Of the 83 leopard samples, six were successfully genotyped at the 18 loci whereas four were genotyped at 11-17 loci giving a total of ten multilocus genotypes. Comparison of these ten genotypes resulted in the identification of seven unique individuals (three males and four females). The number of mismatched loci in each pair of the seven individuals ranged from 6 to 18. The error rates per locus fell within the range of 0-0.33 for allelic dropout and 0-0.06 for false alleles, and the average error rates for allelic dropout and false alleles were 0.14 and 0.01, respectively. Therefore we considered that three amplifications could effectively minimize genotyping errors. There were no significant deviations from Hardy-Weinberg or linkage equilibrium at the 18 loci. Only the five most informative loci decreased the probability of identity for siblings (P ID-sib ) to less than 0.01, a threshold required for reliable abundance estimation (Waits et al., 2001). A further increase of the number of loci up to 8-10 decreased the P ID-sib to the level of 0.001-0.0001, which may be useful in cases of parentage analysis or forensic analysis. Microsatellite markers we examined achieved a sufficient level of accuracy for individual identification. Depending on the objectives of noninvasive genetic sampling, such as abundance estimation, genetic structure, or relatedness, one can select the appropriate number of markers from these 18 loci. It should be noted, however, that, because our genetic diversity statistics are based on 5-7 individuals found in eastern Cambodia, our results should not be treated as representative of this subspecies. When a noninvasive genetic sampling for abundance estimation is initiated in other geographic area, it would be better to test the selected loci, especially the 5-8 most informative loci (Table 2), and to reestimate the P ID-sib .
Given the low level of genotyping success rate (12%, 10/83), improving the success rate is necessary for future studies. The low success rate is partially because during field sampling all leopard fecal samples encountered were collected regardless of age and quality. Even old and desiccated samples were useful to reveal leopard distribution, but they were not successfully genotyped. Preservation method is also likely to be associated with the low amplification success rate. We used DMSO buffer, but 70% or 100% ethanol preservations may be worth considering given the higher success rates in other studies in tropical or subtropical regions (e.g. Dutta et al., 2012;Mondol et al., 2009).
The present study is the first to show genetic diversity at the multiple microsatellite loci of wild leopard in South-east Asia. Despite the documented population decline of leopards in Cambodia during the 20th century (Loucks et al., 2009), the genetic diversity we document was high. The population decline in the last century might not have entailed an apparent loss of genetic diversity, although a further assessment with a larger sample size over a larger proportion of this subspecies range is needed.
